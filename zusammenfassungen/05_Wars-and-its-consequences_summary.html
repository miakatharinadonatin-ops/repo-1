The Economics of Wars and Their Consequences

Economic History · Lecture 6 · 16 October 2026 · Sources: lecture slides, lecture transcript, Juhász (2018), AER

Big picture
  • WWI was financed by debt. Winners borrowed largely from the USA; the losers were charged reparations.
  • Germany's total burden was about 299% of its 1913 GDP. Its tax revenue could not carry it.
  • The central bank printed money; hyperinflation followed in 1923.
  • Hyperinflations stopped abruptly once expectations changed credibly.

1. War economy and endowment

  • Churchill: 1914–1945 is the "second thirty years' war"; the interwar period was a period without war, not peace (mistrust, no coordination).
  • In WWI France and Germany spent more than 50% of GDP on the war. This was only possible for industrialised economies with a surplus. Today war is relatively cheaper ("bangs per buck", Ferguson 2001).
  • Allies were larger in population, area and GDP. Central Powers could only win by surprise, not with resources (Blitzkrieg logic). Ritschl (2014): for Germany 1914 was the last chance to win.
  • Rathenau's input-output planning model (WWI) was called by Lenin "a complete guide to socialism".
  • Stronger suffering in WWI meant earlier women's suffrage (Germany, Austria: 1918–20; UK 1928; Switzerland 1971).

The London Schedule demanded more than 10% of German GDP per year. Dawes Plan (1924) and Young Plan (1930) lowered payments; Hitler stopped all payments in 1933.

4. German hyperinflation 1921–1923

  • 1920/21: reparations announced → political tension and falling tax revenue.
  • Summer 1921: central bank starts financing the budget by printing money.
  • 1922 budget (million Goldmark): revenues fell from 1,205 (Q1) to 646 (Q4); spending stayed at about 1,500 per quarter; deficit rose from 499 to 826.
  • Quarterly price inflation: 55.8% (Q1), 308.3% (Q3), 419.9% (Q4) 1922.
  • Jan 1923 onwards: France occupied the Ruhr (most industrialised region); the government financed a general strike by printing money → hyperinflation.
  • November 1923: abrupt stop after currency reform (Rentenbank), Dawes Plan.
  • Austria, Hungary and Poland had similar hyperinflations. Poland had no state tax capacity (three tax systems from three empires).

5. Why did hyperinflation stop overnight?

  • Money supply kept growing after the stop (e.g. Austria: monetary base +20–25% per month during hyperinflation; growth continued afterwards). Hence money growth alone cannot explain the stop.
  • Expectations: Lucas (1976) "Inflation is driven by expected inflation". Sargent (1982): the end of hyperinflation is evidence for rational expectations.
  • Adaptive expectations: prices keep rising as in the past; disinflation is slow and costly. Rational expectations: a credible change makes stopping possible immediately (baker example: nobody raises prices if others don't).
  • Credible regime change: League of Nations, independent central banks, binding limits on government borrowing, international loans, gold-exchange standard. Kydland & Prescott (1977): time-inconsistency problem → binding commitment is needed.

6. Fragile stabilisation and the Great Depression

  • 1924 looked stable, but the Dawes Plan was only an interim solution, linked to unresolved war debts.
  • Hot money flowed into Germany and Central Europe.
  • Gold-exchange standard with wrong parities (pound overvalued, France undervalued), foreign currency as transmission channel, gold hoarding → imbalances amplified the Great Depression (next lecture).

7. WWI and women's labour supply

Gay (2023), France:

  • Men per 100 women (aged 15–50) fell from about 98 before WWI to about 89 after it.
  • Counties with more military deaths saw more married women working. Elasticity about 1 (roughly 0.6–1.05) in censuses 1962–2012.
  • Effect transmitted from mothers to daughters, from mothers-in-law to daughters-in-law and via social interaction.

8. Unintended consequences: Napoleonic blockade

Juhász (2018):

  • Continental Blockade (1806): trade with Britain was rerouted via Helgoland, Gothenburg, Gibraltar and Malta. Trade costs rose most in northern France (Seine-Maritime 86 → 1,282 land-km to London), least in the south (Pyrénées-Orientales 548 → 689 km).
  • Question: did heavily protected regions build up cotton spinning? Method: difference-in-differences with spindles per 1,000 inhabitants.
  • Result: 1803–1812 spindles rose from 380,000 to about 1.4 million; regions with higher trade costs expanded more (coefficient 33.47, significant). Placebo industries (wool, leather) show no effect.
  • Long run: one extra spindle in 1812 → 2.1–2.7 more in 1840 and 4.7–4.9 more in 1887. Value added effect only in 1860. Author calls long-run results "suggestive".
  • Mechanism likely learning-by-doing externalities (not tested). Infant industry argument works here because protection was unintended and the conditions (skills) were already in place.
  • Lecture link: sanctions can protect domestic industries (Russian cheese example).
