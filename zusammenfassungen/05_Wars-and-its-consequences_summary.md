## 0. The big picture – how everything fits together

1. **WWI was an exceptionally resource-intensive war:** France and Germany spent around or above 50% of GDP on the war.
2. **The winners (UK, France) went heavily into debt**, largely towards the USA, to finance the war.
3. To repay these debts and to punish Germany, they imposed **reparations (A-, B-, C-reparations)** → Germany's total burden ≈ **299% of its 1913 GDP**.
4. Germany could not pay through taxes or new loans → the **central bank printed money** to finance the budget. Tax revenues collapsed, deficits rose → inflation from 1921/22 → **hyperinflation in 1923**, accelerated by the **French occupation of the Ruhr** and the money-financed general strike.
5. **Austria, Hungary and Poland** went through similar hyperinflations.
6. All four hyperinflations **stopped abruptly, from one day to the next – although the money supply kept growing.** Explanation: **rational expectations** + a **credible regime change** (League of Nations, independent central banks, reparations renegotiation, gold-exchange standard) (Lucas, Sargent; Kydland & Prescott).
7. The **stabilization of 1924 was fragile** (Dawes Plan only an interim solution, hot capital inflows, problems of the gold-exchange standard) → this later amplified the **Great Depression** (next lecture).
8. Wars also have **long-run social consequences** (women's suffrage, female labor force participation in France – Gay 2023) and **unintended economic consequences**: the Napoleonic blockade acted as **infant industry protection** for French cotton spinning (Juhász 2018).

## 1. A snapshot on World War I

### 1.1 The "second Thirty Years' War"

- Winston Churchill called the period **1914–1945 the "second thirty years war"**: WWI + WWII + the interwar period in between.
- The interwar period was **not a real peace, only a period without war**: there was **coordination failure and mistrust** among the European powers (e.g., because of the reparations), no common European policy against the arrival of the Great Depression, and the rise of fascist/autocratic movements.
- **[Background]** The "first" Thirty Years' War (1618–1648) was a long series of wars in Central Europe; Churchill's label suggests that 1914–1945 should be seen as *one* connected conflict.

### 1.2 War capacity: an unprecedented use of economic resources

WWI and WWII were unique in history in their use of economic resources: countries spent **over 50% of their GDP** on the war.

**War expenses in % of GDP during WWI** (Broadberry & Harrison 2005):

| Year | Australia | Canada | France | Germany | UK | USA |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1913 | 5.5 | 7.0 | 10.0 | 9.8 | 8.1 | 1.8 |
| 1914 | 5.7 | 10.0 | 22.3 | 23.9 | 12.7 | 1.9 |
| 1915 | 9.6 | 13.1 | 46.4 | 43.8 | 33.3 | 1.9 |
| 1916 | 14.0 | 16.5 | 47.2 | 50.3 | 37.1 | 1.5 |
| 1917 | 17.2 | 15.7 | 49.9 | 59.0 | 37.1 | 3.2 |
| 1918 | 17.2 | 16.9 | 53.5 | 50.1 | 35.1 | 16.6 |

**Why were these shares only possible in WWI/WWII?**
- **Before 1914 impossible:** countries were poor and resources scarce. A poor society needs most of its output (lecture: 70–80%) just to feed its people → only a small surplus can go to war.
- **After 1945 no longer necessary:** "cheap" weapons of mass destruction. Ferguson (2001): *"In terms of bangs per buck, military technology has never been cheaper."* Rich economies today do not need to spend such GDP shares to fight a war (lecture example: Russia today spends no more than roughly 15–20% of GDP even though it is fighting heavily).
- **WWI was the "sweet spot":** industrialization created a large surplus that could be allocated to war, but countries were not yet so rich that war became "cheap" relative to GDP.

### 1.3 The economic and political situation before WWI

- **Conflicts between the major European powers increased after 1880.**
  - **Divergence in economic development:** the UK and Germany grew (population, GDP, technology), while France, Austria-Hungary, the Ottoman Empire and Russia fell back (in relative terms).
  - **Germany claimed colonies** (land, natural resources, markets) – Germany was a late starter in the race for colonies (e.g., in Africa).
- **Increasing tensions *within* the major powers:** nationalist and social conflicts in Austria-Hungary, Russia and Germany (lecture: e.g., Slavic peoples in Austria-Hungary striving for independence – think of Czechs, Slovaks, Croats).
- **Trigger:** Balkan conflicts and loyalty to the alliances. The assassination of Franz Ferdinand in Sarajevo (1914) → Austria-Hungary gave Serbia an ultimatum → Serbia did not comply → war Austria-Hungary vs. Serbia → Russia joined to help Serbia → Germany joined to help Austria-Hungary → intervention of Germany, Great Britain, France (thereafter: USA and Italy).
  - **[Note]** The transcript calls Franz Ferdinand the "son of the Kaiser". **[Background]** He was the heir to the Austro-Hungarian throne (nephew of Emperor Franz Joseph).
- **Ritschl (2014):** For Germany, starting the war in 1914 was the **last chance to win it** → therefore **no German de-escalation policy after Sarajevo**. Lecture: Germany was on a high industrialization path and expected that France and Russia would catch up industrially within 10–20 years.

### 1.4 The economics of war: endowment – who wins a long war?

- **Winning a war depends on economic resources and their mobilization for the military.** The side with the larger endowment is more likely to win a long-lasting war (lecture: this also applies to the Russia–Ukraine war today).
- Important variables:
  - **Population** → number of soldiers,
  - **Land area** → natural resources (coal, iron, oil …),
  - **GDP** → resources that can be allocated to the war,
  - **GDP per capita** → technology, possibility of mass production.

**Resources of the Allies relative to the Central Powers (Central Powers = 1)** (Broadberry & Harrison 2005). Central Powers = mainly Germany and Austria-Hungary; Allies = mainly France, UK, Russian Empire, later USA.

| | Population | Total area | Area per capita | GDP | GDP per capita |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Nov 1914 – total | 5.2 | 11.5 | 2.2 | 2.9 | 0.6 |
| Nov 1914 – major powers only | 2.2 | 19.4 | 8.8 | 1.8 | 0.8 |
| Nov 1916 – total | 5.5 | 12.1 | 2.2 | 3.2 | 0.6 |
| Nov 1916 – major powers only | 2.2 | 19.4 | 8.8 | 1.8 | 0.8 |
| Nov 1918 – total | 8.1 | 13.5 | 1.7 | 4.6 | 0.6 |
| Nov 1918 – major powers only | 1.6 | 7.5 | 4.8 | 2.5 | 1.6 |

([Note] The slide title says "1914, 1946 and 1918"; the table shows November 1916, so "1946" is a typo.)

**Interpretation:**
- The Allies were larger in population, area and GDP throughout the war.
- In **GDP per capita** the Allies were initially *poorer* (0.6 / 0.8), mainly because the relatively poor **Russian Empire** belonged to them.
- By **November 1918** the major Allied powers also had a **higher GDP per capita (1.6)**: Russia had left the war after the **Bolshevik revolution**, and the **USA** had entered.
- **Conclusion:** The Central Powers could win only through **military strategy or surprise, never through their resources**. The same logic explains the **German Blitzkrieg strategy in WWII**: Nazi Germany knew it was too small to win a long war against France and Russia unless it won quickly.

### 1.5 Political consequences of WWI

- **Democratization: extension of the welfare state and female suffrage.**
  - **Welfare state:** the mass mobilization of women and men for military service led to political concessions to the population.
  - The welfare state **changed the attitude towards migration/migrants** ("who belongs to us?"), which provided a **breeding ground for radical ideologies**.
  - **(Promised) female suffrage 1918–1920:** Germany, Czechoslovakia, Hungary, Austria, Ireland, Canada, Poland, Netherlands. Later: **United Kingdom 1928, Switzerland 1971!**
  - **Pattern (lecture):** the more a country suffered in WWI, the earlier women got the right to vote. Losers (Germany, Austria) introduced it right after the war; winners took longer (UK about 10 years); Switzerland, not directly affected by the war, is the extreme case (1971).
- **New countries in Central and Eastern Europe** (e.g., Poland re-established from parts of the Russian Empire, the German Empire and the Habsburg monarchy), which also served as a **buffer between the major powers (Germany vs. USSR)**.

### 1.6 WWI as a playground for "central planning"

- When the state allocates 50–60% of GDP to the war, the state administration expands enormously → the war economy is a kind of **planning economy**.
- **Walther Rathenau** (son of the AEG founder and a leading Siemens manager) designed a **model for a planning economy** (lecture: based on **input-output tables**), which **Lenin (1917)** called *"a complete guide to socialism"*.
- The **Nazis and the GDR further developed these WWI central-planning instruments** (lecture: also used in the Soviet-style command economy).
- **[Background]** An input-output table shows how much each sector of the economy needs from every other sector to produce its output; a planner can use it to calculate which inputs are needed for a given production target.

## 2. Reparations, war debt and hyperinflation

### 2.1 War debt among the Allies

- The European Allies **won the war only with the military and monetary(!) help of the USA.**
- The UK and France financed their war effort by borrowing on financial markets and from the USA → their public debt exploded.
- **The UK and France imposed reparation claims on the Central Powers (especially Germany, Austria and Hungary) to repay their debt to the US.**

**Public debt and reparation claims – Germany, UK and France:**

| | Germany (bn Goldmark) | Germany (% of 1913 GDP) | UK (bn £) | UK (% of 1913 GDP) | France (bn FF) | France (% of 1913 GDP) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Public debt 1913 | 32.8 | 63 | 711 | 33 | 33,637 | 67 |
| Public debt 1920 | 25.2 | **48** | 3,160 | **144** | 66,953 | **135** |
| A-reparations | 10.0 | 19 | | | | |
| B-reparations | 42.0 | 80 | | | | |
| C-reparations | 80.0 | 152 | | | | |
| **Sum of debt** | **157.2** | **299** | | | | |

- **Surprising fact:** Germany's *official* public debt **fell** over the war (63% → 48% of 1913 GDP). Lecture explanation: a large part of the war spending had already been **inflated away** by the higher inflation in Germany.
- The debt of the UK (33% → 144%) and France (67% → 135%) rose dramatically because they borrowed to finance their armament.

### 2.2 The A-, B- and C-reparations

Reparation payments were **resolved in 1920 and fixed in 1921** (to be paid **in kind or in cash**).

| Type | Purpose | Size | Justification |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A-reparations** | Cover the **direct war costs** (destroyed bridges, housing, railway tracks … in France, Belgium, etc.) | 19% of 1913 GDP | Amount comparable to historical payments after defeats; easy to justify (real damages) |
| **B-reparations** | Repay the **war debts of France and GB to the US** ("indirect costs" of the war) | 80% | Justifiable, but much higher than historically because of the **"industrialization of war"** (war had become very expensive) |
| **C-reparations** | **Punishment** and **propaganda for the "home front"** | 152% | Not related to any damage or loan; meant to "keep Germany down" |

→ Total: **≈ 299% of German GDP (1913)** – an extremely high burden (lecture comparison: much higher than the debt levels discussed in the Greek debt crisis or of France and Italy today).

### 2.3 Can Germany pay? Keynes' warning

To pay reparations (in cash or in kind), Germany must earn foreign currency, i.e., run a **current account surplus**:
- **German citizens will suffer** from higher (consumer) taxes and lower wages needed to produce this surplus.
- **Terms of trade will turn against Germany:** prices of the transferred goods fall relative to the prices of imported goods → a trade surplus is even harder to achieve. Lecture: if Germany ships huge amounts of goods abroad as reparations, foreign consumers are already supplied by these deliveries → Germany loses export markets for the goods it produces best → paying becomes even less likely.
- **J. M. Keynes (1919), "The Economic Consequences of the Peace":** *"Germany can never execute this treaty. Either renegotiation or anger and revenge!"*
  - Lecture: **both happened** – there were renegotiations (Dawes Plan, Young Plan), and there was anger and revenge: German voters increasingly voted for the Nazis, who promised revenge for the defeat in WWI and the punishment that followed.

**Repayment scheme for Germany** (Webb 1986; figure in bn Goldmark per year):
- **London Schedule (1921):** initial payment scheme of **more than 10% of German GDP** per year (about 4 bn Goldmark in 1921, planned to rise to about 6.5 bn by 1929).
- **Dawes Plan (from 1924):** much lower payments (rising from about 0.5 to 2.5 bn) – renegotiated after the hyperinflation.
- **Young Plan (from 1930):** further reduction (about 2 bn) – during the Great Depression.
- **Hitler stopped all payments** (arrow on the slide at 1933).
- Cartoon on the slide: a tiny man ("Germany") under a giant sack "Reparations $55,000,000,000" – "Will Germany survive?" (Eichengreen 1992).

### 2.4 Germany: three options to pay reparations

States have three options to meet their payment liabilities (in domestic or foreign currency):

**(1) Higher (consumer) taxes / VAT and higher costs for public services**
- Higher consumer taxes reduce demand for domestic *and* foreign goods → **trade surplus** increases. (Higher taxes on *labor* would be a "no go": they would also raise export prices.)
- Lecture: consumption taxes do not destroy work incentives, but they make consumption more expensive → people keep producing but consume less → trade surplus.
- The trade/current account surplus is used to repay the debt → the **"typical" IMF recommendation** (lecture: e.g., the Troika in the Greek debt crisis).
- But who pays? **Consumers will oppose this option.**

**(2) Issue new debt to pay the reparations (debt rescheduling)**
- Repay old bonds with newly issued bonds.
- But **who will lend money to Germany** (with debt of ~300% of GDP), and **at what price (interest rate)?**
- Furthermore, Germany needed **FDI** after WWI: who will invest in such an environment?

**(3) Print money to pay the reparations / repay debt**
- Printing money **above the growth rate of the economy** increases the risk of inflation.
- Printing money is **also feasible if the debt is denominated in foreign currency**, due to **seigniorage** (revenue from additional money at given short-term exchange rates). Lecture: the central bank can print new money overnight, and the state can buy foreign currency at the *old* exchange rate before inflation and depreciation set in.
- **The problems of inflation:**
  1. it affects exchange rates (**devaluation** of the currency);
  2. **imports become more expensive** (unhappy consumers);
  3. inflation **reduces the value of tax revenues from t−1** (taxes assessed earlier are paid later in devalued money);
  4. inflation eventually leads to **capital flight** (and hoarding of value-preserving assets).
- Inflation also reduces confidence in monetary policy and the central bank.
- **Thus: printing money as a last resort!!!**
- **[Background]** *Seigniorage* = the real resources the state gains by issuing new money (the difference between the value of money and its production cost).

### 2.5 The hyperinflation in Germany

**What is hyperinflation?**
- A fast devaluation of money (equivalent: a rapid increase in the price level).
- Rule of thumb (Wikipedia): hyperinflation if **prices increase by more than 50% per month** (> 13,000% per year).
- **[Background]** 1.5¹² ≈ 130, i.e., 50% per month compounds to a price increase of roughly 13,000% per year.

**The situation in Germany until 1922/23:**
- After WWI, Germany **stabilized** politically and economically and implemented a **successful tax reform**: increasing tax revenues, falling unemployment, stabilizing prices, economic growth.
- **1920/21:** the **reparation payments were announced** → political tensions, violence, and finally **reduced tax revenues**. Lecture: once Germans saw how much money had to be sent abroad, they lost confidence in the state, and the willingness to pay taxes fell.
- **Summer 1921:** the **central bank started to finance the budget by printing money** → start of inflationary pressure and depreciation of the exchange rate.

**The German budget in 1922 (in million Goldmark) and the start of (hyper)inflation** (Eichengreen 1992):

| Quarter | Revenues | Spending | Deficit | Inflation (%, quarterly change in WPI) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1922-I | 1,205 | 1,703 | 499 | 55.8 |
| 1922-II | 1,293 | 1,590 | 297 | 29.4 |
| 1922-III | **888** | 1,473 | 585 | **308.3** |
| 1922-IV | **646** | 1,472 | 826 | **419.9** |

- Revenues **fell by almost half** (1,205 → 646, −46% from Q1 to Q4; the lecture rounds this to "more than 50%"), while spending fell only slightly (the state still had to function).
- The **deficit** (the residual) therefore rose, and it was financed with newly printed money → quarterly inflation of **308% and 420%**.
- Lecture: this was the *start* of inflation, not yet the core hyperinflation.

**The year 1923:**
- Germany was **no longer able to pay the reparations** → **France invaded the Ruhr region** (the most industrialized German region; lecture: up to 40% of German GDP, coal mines, heavy industry, iron smelters, firms such as Thyssen and Krupp; cities like Duisburg, Essen, Dortmund, Bottrop) **to get the reparations in kind**.
- The German government **co-organized a general strike financed by the central bank** (workers were paid with newly printed money so that they would not work for the French occupiers) → **hyperinflation!**

**Timeline of the German price level 1919–1924** (Sargent 1982; log scale):
1. Initial stabilization (end of 1919/1920),
2. Announcement of reparations (1921),
3. Central bank starts to print money for the budget (1921),
4. France occupies the Ruhr; central bank finances the general strike (1923),
5. Hyperinflation (1923),
6. **November 1923: abrupt stop of hyperinflation.**
7. After November 1923: **currency reform, Dawes Plan, stabilization of the budget and the economy.**

- **[Background]** On a log scale, equal vertical distances mean equal *growth factors*. On this chart each labelled step of the axis (1, 10³, 10⁶, 10⁹, 10¹², 10¹⁵) is a factor of **1,000**. **[Note]** In the lecture the log scale was described as "each step is a doubling"; the axis labels on the slide show powers of ten.

### 2.6 Four hyperinflations and their ends

| Country | Main causes (slide) | Approx. end (shaded on slides) |
|---|---|---|
| **Germany** | Reparations, Ruhr occupation | November 1923 |
| **Austria** | Reparations, foreign debt | Autumn 1922 |
| **Hungary** | Reparations, foreign debt | Early 1924 |
| **Poland** | War with the USSR, 3 different tax systems | Early 1924 |

- Austria and Hungary were also losers of WWI and had to pay reparations (smaller than Germany's).
- **Poland** is different: re-established after WWI out of parts of the German Reich, the Habsburg Empire and the Russian Empire (hence 3 different tax systems) → **no state capacity to tax** → the central bank financed the state → hyperinflation.

**The general pattern:**
- Inflation was dramatic but followed a similar pattern: **massive budget deficit → printing money → inflation → depreciation of the currency.**
- **But: hyperinflation stopped suddenly, more or less from one day to the next.**
- Lecture contrast: after the inflation of the 1970s or after Covid, it took a long time until prices stabilized.

### 2.7 The puzzle: money supply kept growing after the stop

Sargent (1982) data on **note circulation**: red = hyperinflation, green = after the stop.
- **Austria:** during hyperinflation the monetary base grew by roughly 20–25% per month (lecture). It continued to grow strongly after the stop (e.g., Aug 1922: 1,353,403,632 thousand crowns → Dec 1922: 4,080,177,238), and roughly doubled again by the end of 1924 – while prices stayed stable.
- **Hungary:** notes in circulation rose from 1,278,437 (Feb 1924) to 4,513,990 (Dec 1924) after the stop.
- **Poland:** note circulation rose from 313,659,830 (Jan 1924) to 528,913,418.7 (Feb 1924) and 570,697,550.5 (Apr 1924).
- **Germany:** note circulation even **skyrocketed** in the weeks after the stop (15 Nov 1923: 92,844,720,743 → 30 Nov 1923: 400,267,640,302 → Dec 1923: 496,507,424,772).

**Why is this a puzzle?** Lecture: the price level is the monetary base divided by the goods and services this money is spread over. If money grows faster than the goods produced, prices rise. If only the money supply mattered, hyperinflation should **not** have stopped – but it did.
- **[Background]** This is the quantity theory of money: M · V = P · Y (money × velocity = price level × real output).

### 2.8 The drivers of the sudden stop: expectations

- **Lucas (1976):** *"Inflation is driven by expected inflation; to end it, change factors that drive expectations."*
- **Sargent (1982):** *"Hyperinflation and its end provides strong evidence in favor of rational expectations."*
- **Key issue: How do agents (firms, consumers) form expectations about inflation?**

| | **Adaptive expectations** | **Rational expectations** |
|---|---|---|
| Idea | Short-sighted: you expect the future to be like the past/present | Agents understand the mechanism that drives the price level |
| Consequence | **High persistence** of inflation; a change in fiscal/monetary policy reduces inflation only **slowly** → disinflation is **costly** | A change in that mechanism can have an **immediate effect** on expectations and inflation → policy changes **might be cheap** |
| Example | US in the mid-1970s: rising inflation; Keynesian arguments (Phillips curve, 1958, 1960) implied high costs of reducing inflation | End of the four hyperinflations in the 1920s |

**The baker example (lecture):**
- *Adaptive:* a baker has experienced that the price of bread doubles every day. So in the morning she already charges twice as much as yesterday – that is what the past tells her.
- *Rational:* if something believable happens and the baker has good reasons to think that **everybody else also believes "today is different"**, then she will not raise her price: if she were the only one doubling her price, she would not sell any bread. If everyone reasons this way, prices stop rising **immediately** – even if the money supply has not (yet) changed.

**What made people believe "today is different"?** It had become obvious to all partner countries and to the **League of Nations** (predecessor of the United Nations) that the situation was unsustainable. It was announced that League of Nations experts would enter these countries, **separate the central bank from the government** and restore a stable monetary policy, and that the **reparations would be renegotiated**. Lecture comparison: today, when the IMF announces it will help a country in trouble, this can change expectations in the same way. Modern central bankers therefore pay great attention to managing expectations.

- **[Background]** The **Phillips curve** describes a negative relationship between inflation and unemployment. In the Keynesian reading of the 1970s, reducing inflation therefore required a costly period of higher unemployment.

### 2.9 The four hyperinflations ended due to regime change

- **Germany:** **currency reforms** and foundation of the **Rentenbank** with **binding limits on government borrowing and the issue of money** (the new currency was backed by land and other assets).
  - Accompanied by the new **Dawes Plan** on reparations, which **lowered the immediate as well as the total burden** of reparations,
  - **intervention by the League of Nations**,
  - a **large international loan and FDI**.
  - **[Background]** The new Rentenmark was introduced in November 1923 at a rate of 1 Rentenmark = 1 trillion (10¹²) paper marks.
- **Austria, Hungary, Poland** stabilized their currencies with **support from the League of Nations, international loans and monetary reform (partial independence of central banks).**
  - For Poland: the **peace settlement with the USSR** was crucial.
- **All four countries entered the gold-exchange standard with fixed exchange rates.** This **increased the credibility** of the central banks/monetary policy and made **FDI more likely** (a commitment device).
- **Is there any rationale behind the regime change?** → **Kydland & Prescott (1977): "The time inconsistency problem."**
  - **[Background]** Time inconsistency: a government that can print money at its discretion has an incentive to break an announced low-inflation promise later (e.g., to finance deficits). People anticipate this, so a mere promise is not credible. The solution is a **binding commitment** that removes the discretion: an independent central bank, legal limits on lending to the government, a fixed exchange rate / gold standard, international supervision. This is why the 1920s regime changes were credible and could change expectations overnight.

### 2.10 Fragile stabilization in 1924

**1924 looked better than it was:**
- Everything looked well in 1924: political stabilization, price stabilization, economic upturn, better (but still imperfect) conditions for Germany.
- **But key problems were postponed, not resolved:**
  - The **Dawes Plan was only an interim solution** to calm the waters and find a new reparations settlement; it was linked to the **open issue of war debt** (the Allies' debts to the US).
  - Stabilization triggered a wave of **short-term ("hot") capital inflows** into Central Europe and Germany. **[Background]** Hot money = short-term capital that can be withdrawn quickly when confidence falls.
- **The gold-exchange standard was inherently problematic, because:**
  - **parities did not match market rates** (the pound was overvalued; France was still struggling in 1924–26 and entered the gold standard at an undervalued rate). Lecture: the parities did not reflect productivity gains → imbalances in the second half of the 1920s;
  - the **backing of currencies by foreign exchange** was a **new transmission channel** (of shocks);
  - **lack of central bank cooperation (gold hoarding)**, especially in countries with inflationary experience;
  - **new political role of the working class:** the gold standard leaves **less room for country-specific needs** (as today in the Euro area).
- → These imbalances **amplified the spread of the Great Depression** (topic of the next lecture).

## 3. WWI and women empowerment (Gay, *Economic Journal* 2023)

**Women increased their social standing with war-affectedness:**
- **Right to vote:** mainly and first in countries heavily affected by the war (see 1.5).
- **Economic standing:** lost men gave space for **increased female labor force participation**.

**The example of France:**
- **Gender ratio** (men per 100 women, aged 15–50): about 98–99 before WWI → **about 89 right after WWI** → slow recovery (about 95 by the mid-1930s) → about 100 after WWII. The missing men are war casualties. Lecture: in the cohorts of working-age soldiers (about 20–35 years), roughly 40–45% of the men did not return.
- **Military death rates** varied strongly across French counties (départements): from **< 10% to > 20%** → regional variation that can be used for the analysis.

**Results:**
- **Counties with higher casualty rates immediately brought more women into the labor force.**
- To exclude effects of the local labor market structure, the study only looks at **"migrating women"**: women residing and working in region *i* but born in region *j*.
- **The effect persists until today** and is transmitted
  1. from **mothers to daughters**,
  2. from **mothers-in-law to daughters-in-law via their sons**,
  3. through **local social interactions**.
- **Main graph:** elasticities of the casualty rate on women at work (married women aged 30–49, censuses 1962–2012). The estimates lie between about **0.6 and 1.05** and are clearly above zero in every census year.
- **Lecture interpretation:** an elasticity of about 1 means that if the share of missing men rises by one percentage point, female labor force participation also rises by about one percentage point – a missing man was, in a sense, "replaced" by a woman at the workplace. Because these are data from the 1960s–2010s, the affected women are the **daughters and granddaughters** of the war generation → the war shock had **long-lasting effects on gender norms**: seeing your mother work makes you more likely to work as well.

## 4. (Un)intended consequences of wars – Juhász (AER 2018)

### 4.1 Can wars be good for a country?

- **Conflicts often affect the economy:**
  - *International wars:* destruction, re-allocation of resources, shock to import-export linkages, etc.
  - *Sanctions against regimes:* specific persons or industries are sanctioned.
- **Potential positive effects of wars and sanctions, if any:**
  - **Large investments into R&D:** e.g., radar and nuclear power. Lecture: in WWII, mainly the USA invested heavily; the **Manhattan Project** produced the knowledge for the atomic bomb, later also used to produce electricity in nuclear power plants.
  - **Certain industries might benefit:** e.g., **Russian cheese factories started to produce French cheese**. Lecture: because of sanctions after the start of the Russia–Ukraine war, Western countries (e.g., France, Switzerland) may no longer deliver cheese to Russia → Russian producers get a chance to learn to make French/Swiss-style cheese; if they become good enough, they might later even compete in Western markets.
- **Mechanism:** if a sanctioned/blockaded country can no longer import goods, this acts like a **tariff / infant industry protection** → domestic producers get a "window of opportunity" to build up their own industry.

### 4.2 The infant industry argument and why it is hard to test

- **Infant industry argument:** some industries can become competitive in the long run if they receive **temporary trade protection**. It goes back to at least **Alexander Hamilton (1791)** and **Friedrich List (1856)** (both advocated protection for nascent cotton industries in the US and Germany).
- **The estimation of the "causal effect" of infant industry protection is almost impossible:**
  1. **Protected industries are not randomly chosen** by the government (selection/"sorting"); success also depends on how well the policy is implemented.
  2. **No counterfactual:** infant industry policies target the entire country → in case of success, we do not know whether the protected industry would have become successful anyway.
- Lecture: many real-world infant industry policies **failed** (governments chose the wrong industries, made wrong policies, inefficiencies of government plans). The Napoleonic blockade is a case of **unintended** infant industry protection → a **natural experiment** that avoids these problems.

### 4.3 Historical background and setting

- **Napoleonic Wars (1803–1815):** Napoleon conquered (directly or indirectly controlled) most of Continental Europe. **Britain**, his main enemy, **remained unaffected** on its island and had naval superiority (by 1800, twice as many warships as France).
- **The Continental Blockade** was declared by Napoleon in **Berlin in late 1806**: its aim was to **weaken Britain economically** by stopping British goods from entering Continental Europe. Ports were closed to ships carrying British goods, and the military patrolled the coastline. Already **1803–1806**, after France occupied Hanover, **Britain imposed a tight blockade** of the North Sea coast (extended in 1804 to the French Channel and North Sea ports).
  - **[Note]** The slide phrases it as "Britain … aimed at hurting France with a trade embargo", and the lecture describes Britain forbidding its ships to deliver goods to France. According to the paper, the Continental Blockade was Napoleon's policy, with British blockade measures before and alongside it. For the argument, what matters is the same: **trade between Britain and France was massively disrupted.**
- **Before the embargo:** France imported **mainly textiles (cotton yarn) from Britain** due to its weak industrial base.
  - Britain had mechanized cotton spinning in the late 18th century; France lagged far behind (1790: about **900 spinning jennies in France vs. about 18,000 in Britain**). At the beginning of the blockade, French machine-spun yarn sold in France cost at least double the price of British machine-spun yarn in Britain.
  - Britain **prohibited the export of spinning machinery and the emigration of skilled workers until 1843** → the French lacked the tacit, learning-by-doing knowledge.
  - Mechanized cotton spinning was a key industry of the Industrial Revolution (25% of British industrial TFP growth 1780–1860, Crafts 1985) and the first industry to move to **factory production**.
- **In practice, the blockade did not stop trade but diverted it to longer and more expensive routes** (smuggling): via **Helgoland** and **Gothenburg** in the north and **Gibraltar** and **Malta** in the Mediterranean (maps based on ship data from Lloyd's List: before the blockade many ships went directly to northern French/European ports; during the blockade direct shipping to the French Empire practically disappeared).
- **The embargo affected French regions differently:**
  - Napoleon was militarily strong in the north, while Britain controlled the Mediterranean ("a British sea"); from 1808 the **Spanish insurgency** opened the whole Iberian peninsula to British trade.
  - **North/northwest France:** trade distance to Britain increased heavily (they were close to Britain before; now goods had to come via Helgoland and long overland routes). Example: **Seine-Maritime (Rouen): 86 → 1,282 land-km** effective distance to London (goods went via Helgoland, overland down to Strasbourg and back north).
  - **Southern and central France:** relatively less affected (they were already far away; routes changed little). Example: **Pyrénées-Orientales (Perpignan): 548 → 689 land-km** (via Gibraltar and overland from Barcelona).
  - On average, effective distance to London rose from **380 to 1,055 land-km**.
- **Trade cost shock** = log change in the shortest effective distance to London (map: values from 0.00 to 2.90, darkest in the north). **[Note]** The map legend shows *log changes in effective distance*, not price multiples. **[Background]** A log change of 1 corresponds to a factor of about 2.7, a log change of 2.9 to a factor of about 18.
- Lecture interpretation: higher transport costs for British textiles = higher import prices for French consumers = **protection** for French producers, much stronger in the north.

### 4.4 Data and empirical strategy

- **How to measure industrialization due to the embargo:** the **number of mechanized cotton spindles per thousand inhabitants** (department level).
  - Mechanized cotton spindles are a good measure of **physical capital** in the textile industry.
  - Spindles were heavily used in British textile industries at that time, but less so in France.
  - Data collected by the author from handwritten industrial surveys: **1803 (before) and 1812 (end of the blockade)**; 88 departments observed in both years; population held fixed at its 1811 level.
- **Trade cost shock:** constructed from **Lloyd's List** shipping data (1787–1814) – shortest route to London before vs. during the blockade (sea transport is cheaper: 1 sea-km = 0.15 land-km).
- **Difference-in-differences (DiD) with continuous treatment intensity:** compare industrialization in heavily vs. less heavily affected French regions **before and after/during** the embargo:
  - S_it = α_i + δ_t + γ · ln D_it + ε_it
  - S_it = spindles per 1,000 inhabitants in department *i* at time *t*; ln D_it = log effective distance to London; α_i = department fixed effects; δ_t = time fixed effects; **γ** = effect of protection (expected > 0).
- **Identifying assumption:** no other shock at the same time that is correlated with the trade cost shock.
- **[Background]** DiD compares the *change* over time in more-treated regions with the *change* in less-treated regions. Department fixed effects remove all time-constant differences between regions (e.g., geography); time fixed effects remove shocks common to all regions.

### 4.5 Short-run results (during the blockade)

- Mechanized spinning capacity in the French Empire rose by about **370% (from 380,000 to about 1.4 million spindles) between 1803 and 1812**, but very unevenly: strong growth along the **English Channel** (by 1812 the largest spinning department was Seine-Maritime), stagnation or even decline in the south (many modern firms went bankrupt). In 1803 the largest spinning department had been Rhône (around Lyon) in the south.
- **DiD estimate (slides, Juhász Table 1):** effective distance coefficient **33.47** (standard error 9.80) without controls and **38.82** (13.23) with all controls → large, positive and statistically significant.
  - Moving from the 25th to the 75th percentile of the trade cost shock (about 400 land-km – like moving a department from the English Channel to the Spanish border) raises spinning capacity by about the **mean spinning capacity of 1812**.
- **Robust to controls** for locational advantages (each interacted with 1812): streams (water power), coal, market potential (access to urban population), knowledge access (universities), literacy. Also robust to France's changing market access (annexations, satellite states).
- **Other checks:**
  - The price of imported **raw cotton** rose similarly in north and south → it does not explain the results.
  - **No pre-trend** (1794–1803) once market potential is controlled for.
  - No effect on the **capital-labor ratio** or the **type of machines** → no differential factor-price shock or quality upgrading.
  - **Placebo industries:** no effect on **wool spinning** or **leather tanning** (less traded with Britain, no technological change) → the mechanism is reduced import competition from the technological leader, Britain.
  - Growth came mostly from **new firms entering** (extensive margin).
  - Napoleon did **not** support cotton (the army used wool; he offered a prize for a flax-spinning machine and taxed raw cotton imports) → not state support.

### 4.6 Long-run results (after the blockade)

- **Long-run effects:** the north remained the **textile hotspot of France for almost a century** (maps of spindles 1840 and 1887).
- **Method:** instrumental variables (2SLS) – spinning capacity in 1812 is instrumented with the trade cost shock (to use only the variation caused by the protection).
  - **[Background]** An instrument is a variable that affects the outcome only through the explanatory variable of interest; here the trade cost shock should affect later spinning only through the spinning capacity built up during the blockade.
- **Persistence:** one more spindle in 1812 (caused by protection) → about **2.1–2.7 more spindles in 1840** and about **4.7–4.9 more spindles in 1887**. Regions with a first-mover advantage kept expanding as the French industry grew (about 1 million spindles in 1812 → 3 million in 1840 → almost 5 million in 1887).
- The **southern departments shrank in absolute terms** → evidence against the alternative explanation of slow technology diffusion from Britain (then the south should have caught up).
- **Productivity:** industrial value added per capita was **positively shaped for several decades** – significant in **1860**, no longer significant from 1896 on. No effect on agriculture or services.
- **Exports:** before the Napoleonic Wars France was a **net importer** of cotton goods; by the end of the blockade a **net exporter**; by 1828, 7.5% of French exports were cotton textiles. Other Continental countries (outside the former French Empire) still had much smaller cotton spinning industries around 1850.
- **Caveats (paper):** the author views the long-run results as more **suggestive** than the short-run results (over such a long period other shocks cannot be fully ruled out). After 1815, France kept tariffs/prohibitions on British cotton goods. The likely mechanism is **learning-by-doing externalities** (supported by historical evidence, not tested econometrically). The paper does **not** claim that the blockade increased welfare (consumers paid higher prices; the blockade had many other negative effects), and notes that infant industry protection can be a "blunt tool" where the underlying conditions are not in place.

### 4.7 Main take-aways (slides and lecture)

- **French regions that were heavily exposed to the trade embargo built up their own textile industries during the blockade**, and these regions **remained more "industrialized" for another 50 years** and longer.
- **Strong evidence for "infant industry protection" to spur industrialization** – one of the few examples where it worked, and it worked precisely because the protection was **unintended** (no government picking the industry).
- Wars and sanctions can therefore have unintended positive effects for the targeted economy (cf. the Russian cheese example).

## 5. Connections and exam checklist

**Key causal chains to be able to explain:**
1. *Industrialization* → large surplus → war spending > 50% of GDP (WWI) → *state planning* (Rathenau, input-output tables).
2. *Endowment* (population, area, GDP, GDP per capita) → who wins a long war → Central Powers could only win by surprise → Blitzkrieg logic.
3. *Allied war debt to the US* → B-reparations; *punishment* → C-reparations → German burden ≈ 299% of GDP → Keynes: "renegotiation or anger and revenge".
4. *Three ways to pay* (taxes/current account surplus, new debt, printing money) → only printing money was feasible → *falling tax revenues + rising deficit + money finance* → inflation 1922 → *Ruhr occupation + money-financed strike* → hyperinflation 1923.
5. *Sudden stop despite growing money supply* → *rational expectations* (Lucas, Sargent) + *credible regime change* (League of Nations, independent central banks, Rentenbank, Dawes Plan, gold-exchange standard; Kydland & Prescott's time inconsistency).
6. *Fragile 1924 stabilization* (Dawes Plan interim, hot money, gold-exchange standard with wrong parities) → amplified the Great Depression.
7. *War casualties* → fewer men → more women in the labor force → persistent change in gender norms (Gay 2023); suffering → earlier women's suffrage.
8. *Napoleonic blockade* → regionally different rise in trade costs with Britain → unintended infant industry protection → cotton spinning in the north → persistence for decades (Juhász 2018).
